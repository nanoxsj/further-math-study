4.3 Chi-squared tests

Further Probability & Statistics · contingency tables · goodness of fit

CIE 9231 Paper 4 chi-squared tests，依据 John du Feu Further Probability & Statistics。
重要

本章保留教材 Examples 和 Paper 4 真题的原始英文题干；答案按对应 Mark Scheme 分点列出。

1. Core knowledge

两类卡方检验

检验 目的 H_0 的典型写法
Contingency-table test 两个分类变量是否独立 / 是否有关联 “A is independent of B”
Goodness-of-fit test 观察频数是否符合给定的概率模型 “The data fit the stated distribution / model”

两类检验的备择假设均为 “not independent” 或 “do not fit”。卡方检验只能说明是否有 association 或模型是否合适，不能说明因果关系。

计算 expected frequencies

For a contingency table, f_e=\frac{(\text{row total})(\text{column total})}{\text{grand total}},\qquad \chi^2=\sum\frac{(f_o-f_e)^2}{f_e}.

For a goodness-of-fit test, calculate f_e=N\operatorname{P}(X=x). For an open final class, use the full tail probability, e.g. f_e(\ge6)=N\operatorname{P}(X\ge6)=N[1-\operatorname{P}(X\le5)], not just N\operatorname{P}(X=6).

Do not round expected frequencies before calculating contributions. The observed frequencies are integers; expected frequencies normally are not.

Degrees of freedom and combining classes

For an m\times n contingency table, v=(m-1)(n-1).

For a goodness-of-fit test, v=(\text{number of classes after combining})-(\text{number of estimated parameters})-1.

Every expected frequency used in the test should be at least 5. Combine adjacent categories before calculating \chi^2; combine their observed and expected frequencies first, then calculate one contribution for the combined class. Estimating \lambda for a Poisson model or p for a binomial model costs one degree of freedom. A parameter supplied by the question does not.

Full method and conclusion

  1. State H_0 and H_1 in context.
  2. Calculate expected frequencies and show enough working to identify the model or marginal totals used.
  3. Combine classes where necessary.
  4. Calculate and sum \dfrac{(f_o-f_e)^2}{f_e}.
  5. State the degrees of freedom, critical value and significance level.
  6. Compare and give a contextual conclusion: “There is sufficient evidence …” or “There is insufficient evidence …”.

The critical region is always in the right tail: reject H_0 only when the calculated \chi^2 is greater than the critical value.

常见失分点

  • Do not use observed frequencies in place of expected frequencies.
  • Do not calculate degrees of freedom from the original number of classes after categories have been combined.
  • A very small contribution is valid; it means observed and expected frequencies are close.
  • “Accept H_0” should be followed by an evidence-based conclusion, not “the variables are definitely independent”.

2. Textbook Examples

Example 3.1

The 4\times3 contingency table shows the type of car (saloon, sports, hatchback or SUV) owned by 360 randomly chosen people, and the age category (under 30, 30–60, over 60) into which the owners fall.

Observed frequency f_o Under 30 30–60 Over 60 Total
Saloon 10 67 57 134
Sports car 19 14 3 36
Hatchback 32 47 34 113
SUV 7 56 14 77
Total 68 184 108 360

(i) Write down appropriate hypotheses for a test to investigate whether type of car and owner’s age are independent.

(ii) Calculate expected frequencies assuming that the null hypothesis is true.

(iii) Calculate the value of the test statistic X^2.

(iv) Find the critical value at the 5% significance level.

(v) Complete the test.

(vi) Comment on how the ownership of different types of car depends on the age of the owner.

Show answer

(i) H_0:\text{ Car type is independent of owner’s age}, H_1:\text{ Car type is not independent of owner’s age}.

(ii) Using f_e=(\text{row total})(\text{column total})/360 gives

Expected frequency f_e Under 30 30–60 Over 60
Saloon 25.311 68.489 40.200
Sports car 6.800 18.400 10.800
Hatchback 21.344 57.756 33.900
SUV 14.544 39.356 23.100

(iii) X^2=\sum\frac{(f_o-f_e)^2}{f_e}=66.749.

(iv) v=(4-1)(3-1)=6, so the 5% critical value is 12.59.

(v) Since 66.749>12.59, reject H_0. At the 5% significance level, car type and owner’s age are associated.

(vi) Under-30s own fewer saloons and SUVs, more hatchbacks and many more sports cars than expected. The relatively large remaining contributions show that 30–60-year-olds own more SUVs, and over-60s own more saloons and fewer sports cars than expected.

Example 3.2

The number of telephone calls made to a counselling service is thought to be modelled by the Poisson distribution. Data are collected on the number of calls received during one-hour periods, as shown in the table. Use these data to test at the 5% significance level whether a Poisson model is appropriate.

No. of calls per hour 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 Total
Frequency 6 13 26 14 7 4 0 70
Show answer

H_0:\text{ The number of calls can be modelled by the Poisson distribution}, H_1:\text{ The number of calls cannot be modelled by the Poisson distribution}.

The parameter is estimated from the data: \lambda=\frac{0(6)+1(13)+2(26)+3(14)+4(7)+5(4)}{70}=\frac{155}{70}=2.214.

The expected frequencies are 7.65,16.93,18.74,13.84,7.66,3.39,1.79. Combine the final two classes, giving the following calculation table.

No. of calls, X 0 1 2 3 4 5+
Observed f_o 6 13 26 14 7 4
Expected f_e 7.65 16.93 18.74 13.84 7.66 5.18
(f_o-f_e)^2/f_e 0.3544 0.9126 2.8080 0.0020 0.0567 0.2702

X^2=4.4040.

There are six classes, one estimated parameter and one total-frequency restriction, so v=6-1-1=4. The 5% critical value is 9.488. Since 4.404<9.488, accept H_0. The data are consistent with a Poisson distribution for the number of calls.

Example 3.3

An egg packaging firm has introduced a new box for its eggs. Each box holds six eggs. Unfortunately, it finds that the new box tends to mark the eggs. Data on the number of eggs marked in 100 boxes are collected.

No. of marked eggs 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 Total
No. of boxes, f_o 3 3 27 29 10 7 21 100

It is thought that the distribution may be modelled by the binomial distribution. Carry out a test on the data at the 0.5% significance level to determine whether the data can be modelled by the binomial distribution.

Show answer

H_0:\text{ The number of marked eggs can be modelled by the binomial distribution}, H_1:\text{ The number of marked eggs cannot be modelled by the binomial distribution}.

\bar x=\frac{0(3)+1(3)+2(27)+3(29)+4(10)+5(7)+6(21)}{100}=3.45. Thus 6p=3.45, so p=0.575 and q=0.425.

The expected frequencies for x=0,1,\ldots,6 are 0.59,\ 4.78,\ 16.18,\ 29.19,\ 29.62,\ 16.03,\ 3.61. Combine 0,1 and 5,6.

No. of marked eggs, x 0, 1 2 3 4 5, 6
Observed f_o 6 27 29 10 28
Expected f_e 5.37 16.18 29.19 29.62 19.64
(f_o-f_e)^2/f_e 0.07 7.24 0.00 13.00 3.56
X^2=23.87. There are five classes, one estimated parameter and one total restriction, so v=5-1-1=3. The 0.5% critical value is 12.84. Since 23.87>12.84, reject H_0. The data indicate that the binomial distribution is not an appropriate model for the number of marked eggs.

Example 3.4

It is generally believed that a particular genetic defect is carried by 10% of people. A new and simple test becomes available to determine whether somebody is a carrier of this defect, using a blood specimen. As part of a research project, 100 hospitals are asked to carry out this test anonymously on the next 30 blood samples they take. The results are as follows.

Number of positive tests 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7+
Frequency, f_o 11 29 26 20 9 3 1 1

Do these figures support the model that 10% of people carry this defect, independently of any other condition, at the 5% significance level?

Show answer

H_0:\text{ The model that 10% of people carry this defect is appropriate}, H_1:\text{ The model that 10% of people carry this defect is not appropriate}.

Under H_0, X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(30,0.1). The expected frequencies are 4.24,\ 14.13,\ 22.77,\ 23.61,\ 17.71,\ 10.23,\ 4.74,\ 2.58. Combine 0,1 and 6,7+.

No. of positive tests 0, 1 2 3 4 5 6+
Observed f_o 40 26 20 9 3 2
Expected f_e 18.37 22.77 23.61 17.71 10.23 7.32
(f_o-f_e)^2/f_e 25.47 0.46 0.55 4.28 5.11 3.87
X^2=39.74. There are six classes and one total-frequency restriction, with no parameters estimated, so v=6-0-1=5. The 5% critical value is 11.07. Since 39.74>11.07, reject H_0. The data indicate that the binomial distribution with p=0.1 is not an appropriate model.

2021 May/June · 9231/41 and 9231/42 · Question 2

A driving school employs four instructors to prepare people for their driving test. The allocation of people to instructors is random. For each of the instructors, the following table gives the number of people who passed and the number who failed their driving test last year.

Instructor A Instructor B Instructor C Instructor D Total
Pass 72 42 52 68 234
Fail 33 34 41 58 166
Total 105 76 93 126 400

Test at the 10% significance level whether success in the driving test is independent of the instructor. [7]

Show answer

H_0:\text{ driving-test success is independent of instructor},\qquad H_1:\text{ it is not independent}.

The expected frequencies are

A B C D
Pass 61.425 44.460 54.405 73.710
Fail 43.575 31.540 38.595 52.290

\chi^2=1.8206+0.1361+0.1063+0.4423+2.5664+0.1919+0.1499+0.6235=6.037.

With v=(2-1)(4-1)=3, the 10% critical value is 6.251. Since 6.037<6.251, accept H_0. There is sufficient evidence to suggest that driving-test success is independent of instructor.

Two companies, P and Q, produce a certain type of paint brush. An independent examiner rates the quality of the brushes produced as poor, satisfactory or good. He takes a random sample of brushes from each company. The examiner’s ratings are summarised in the table.

Company Poor Satisfactory Good
P 18 43 64
Q 22 22 31

Test, at the 5% significance level, whether quality of brushes is independent of company. [7]

Show answer

H_0:\text{ brush quality is independent of company},\qquad H_1:\text{ brush quality is not independent of company}.

The expected frequencies are

Company Poor Satisfactory Good
P 25.000 40.625 59.375
Q 15.000 24.375 35.625
\chi^2=1.960+0.139+0.360+3.267+0.231+0.600=6.557. There are (2-1)(3-1)=2 degrees of freedom. The 5% critical value is 5.991. Since 6.557>5.991, reject H_0. There is sufficient evidence that brush quality is associated with company.

2024 October/November · 9231/41 and 9231/43 · Question 3

A statistician believes that the number of telephone calls received by an advice centre in a 10-minute interval can be modelled by the Poisson distribution \operatorname{Po}(1.9). The number of calls received in a randomly chosen 10-minute interval was recorded on each of 100 days. The results are summarised in the table, together with some of the expected frequencies corresponding to \operatorname{Po}(1.9).

Number of calls 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 or more
Observed frequency 10 18 35 21 11 4 1
Expected frequency 14.957 28.418 26.997 p q r 1.322

(a) Complete the table. [2]

(b) Carry out a goodness of fit test, at the 10% significance level, to determine whether the statistician’s belief is reasonable. [6]

Show answer

(a) p=17.098, q=8.121 and r=3.086.

(b) H_0:\operatorname{Po}(1.9)\text{ is a satisfactory model},\qquad H_1:\operatorname{Po}(1.9)\text{ is not a satisfactory model}.

Combine the final three classes: O=16, E=12.529. \chi^2=0.983+3.821+2.372+0.889+0.961=9.03.

There are five classes and one total-frequency restriction, hence v=4. The 10% critical value is 7.779. Since 9.03>7.779, reject H_0. There is sufficient evidence that the statistician’s Poisson model is not reasonable.

2025 May/June · 9231/43 · Question 1

A person’s eye colour may be categorised as “brown”, “blue” or “other”. A scientist claims that these eye colours are uniformly distributed and hence are equally likely to occur in the population. A survey of 120 people from this population found that 38 people had brown eyes, 52 people had blue eyes and 30 people had eyes which were neither brown nor blue.

Use the data to carry out a goodness of fit test at the 5% significance level to test the scientist’s claim. [6]

Show answer

H_0:\text{ eye colours are uniformly distributed},\qquad H_1:\text{ eye colours are not uniformly distributed}.

The expected frequency in each category is 120/3=40. \chi^2=\frac{(38-40)^2}{40}+\frac{(52-40)^2}{40}+\frac{(30-40)^2}{40}=6.20.

With v=3-1=2, the 5% critical value is 5.991. Since 6.20>5.991, reject H_0. There is sufficient evidence that eye colours are not uniformly distributed in the population.

2025 October/November · 9231/41 and 9231/43 · Question 2

The manager of a car park claims that the number of cars entering the car park follows a Poisson distribution with mean 2.8. The numbers of cars entering the car park are recorded on a working day during successive 5-minute periods. The following table contains the observed frequencies, together with most of the expected frequencies and their contributions to the \chi^2 test statistic.

Number of cars 0 1 2 3 4 5 \ge6
Observed frequency 2 15 31 29 13 3 7
Expected frequency 6.081 17.03 23.84 p 15.57 8.721 6.511
\chi^2 contribution 2.739 0.241 2.152 q 0.425 3.753 0.037

(a) Find the value of p and the value of q. [2]

(b) Carry out a goodness of fit test at the 5% significance level to investigate the manager’s claim. [4]

Show answer

(a) p=22.251 and q=2.049.

(b) H_0:\text{ the data fit }\operatorname{Po}(2.8),\qquad H_1:\text{ the data do not fit }\operatorname{Po}(2.8).

Combine the final two classes: O=10, E=15.232, with contribution 1.798. \chi^2=2.739+0.241+2.152+2.049+0.425+1.798=9.404.

There are six classes and one total restriction, so v=5. The 5% critical value is 11.07. Since 9.404<11.07, accept H_0. There is insufficient evidence to reject the manager’s claim.

2022 May/June · 9231/41 and 9231/42 · Question 4

A scientist is investigating the numbers of a particular type of butterfly in a certain region. He claims that the numbers of these butterflies found per square metre can be modelled by a Poisson distribution with mean 2.5. He takes a random sample of 120 areas, each of one square metre, and counts the number of these butterflies in each of these areas. The following table shows the observed frequencies together with some of the expected frequencies using the scientist’s Poisson distribution.

Number per square metre 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 \ge7
Observed frequency 12 20 36 32 13 6 1 0
Expected frequency 9.85 24.63 30.78 25.65 p 8.02 3.34 q

(a) Find the values of p and q, correct to 2 decimal places. [2]

(b) Carry out a goodness of fit test, at the 10% significance level, to test the scientist’s claim. [6]

Show answer

(a) p=16.03 and q=1.70.

(b) H_0:\text{ the data fit }\operatorname{Po}(2.5),\qquad H_1:\text{ the data do not fit }\operatorname{Po}(2.5).

Combine the final two classes: O=1, E=3.34+1.70=5.04. The contributions are 0.4693,\ 0.8704,\ 0.8853,\ 1.5720,\ 0.5727,\ 0.5088,\ 3.2384, so \chi^2=8.12.

There are seven combined classes and one total restriction, hence v=6. The 10% critical value is 10.64. Since 8.12<10.64, accept H_0. There is sufficient evidence to support the scientist’s claim that the data fit a Poisson distribution with mean 2.5.

2023 May/June · 9231/41 and 9231/42 · Question 3

A random sample of 50 values of the continuous random variable X was taken. These values are summarised in the following table.

Interval 1\le x<1.5 1.5\le x<2 2\le x<2.5 2.5\le x<3 3\le x<3.5 3.5\le x\le4
Observed frequency 3 3 8 11 13 12
Expected frequency 4.4271 a 6.1285 8.4549 b 14.9678

It is required to test the goodness of fit of the distribution with probability density function f(x)=\frac1{24}\left(\frac4{x^2}+x\right),\quad 1\le x\le4, and f(x)=0 otherwise.

(a) Show that a=4.6007 and find the value of b. [3]

(b) Carry out a goodness of fit test, at the 10% significance level, to test whether f is a satisfactory model for the data. [6]

Show answer

(a) a=50\int_{1.5}^{2}\frac1{24}\left(\frac4{x^2}+x\right)\,dx=4.6007, and b=11.4211.

(b) Combine the first two classes: O=6, E=4.4271+4.6007=9.0278. \chi^2=1.0155+0.5715+0.7661+0.2183+0.5885=3.16.

H_0:f\text{ is a good fit for the data},\qquad H_1:f\text{ is not a good fit for the data}.

There are five classes after combining and one total restriction, so v=4. The 10% critical value is 7.78. Since 3.16<7.78, accept H_0. There is insufficient evidence to suggest that f is not a good fit for the data.

2023 October/November · 9231/42 · Question 2

The number of breakdowns on a particular section of road is recorded each day over a period of 90 days. It is suggested that the number of breakdowns follows a Poisson distribution with mean 3.5. The data is summarised in the table, together with some of the expected frequencies resulting from the suggested Poisson distribution.

Number of breakdowns per day 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
Observed frequency 0 5 13 17 21 16 9 5
Expected frequency 2.718 9.512 16.646 p 16.993 11.895 q 3.469

(a) Complete the table. [2]

With seven classes and one total restriction, v=6. The 10% critical value is 10.64. Since 10.0<10.64, accept H_0. There is insufficient evidence to suggest that \operatorname{Po}(3.5) is not a good fit to the data.
